Defining Spatial Restraint WithLead Architect Manav Goud Kuncha

The most difficult phase of our turnkey execution is knowing when to stop. We spoke with our lead interior architect about the agonizing process of finalizing a space and resisting the urge to over-saturate the room with artifacts.

The Obsession with Tolerances

“Restraint is not natural,” Cross notes. “When you have access to incredible bespoke furniture and monumental art, the instinct is to fill the void. But luxury is found in the restraint.” For the Basalt Ridge commission, the team removed three distinct sculptural pieces the day before final client handover.

Delivering the Final Environment

The final layer of bespoke curation must act as punctuation, not the entire sentence. Cross believes that if an object can be removed from a room without the spatial energy collapsing, that object never belonged there in the first place.

The Anxiety of the Void

“Clients often experience a profound anxiety when faced with an empty wall,” Cross explains. “They view emptiness as a problem to be solved with art or casework. My job is to convince them that the negative space is the primary feature, and the wall itself is the masterpiece.”

Subtracting the Artifact

During the final curation phase, the studio implements a strict rule of subtraction. For every new artifact or piece of furniture brought into the environment, one must be removed. This mathematical discipline ensures the space never crosses the invisible threshold from curated to cluttered.

Punctuation Over Prose

If the interior architecture is the prose of a novel, the bespoke furniture should be the punctuation. “A room shouldn’t scream at you through loud furnishings,” Cross says. “A single, incredibly heavy, patinated bronze chair placed perfectly in a sunbeam says infinitely more than a room stuffed with luxury sofas.”

The Discipline of Refusal

True restraint requires the discipline to refuse a beautiful object simply because the space does not require it. The studio recently passed on a monumental, museum-grade ceramic sculpture because its placement would have visually compromised a 40-foot unbroken sightline through the primary residence.

Curating Negative Space

We do not curate objects; we curate the air around the objects. Cross spends days during a turnkey execution adjusting a dining table by mere centimeters to ensure the flow of empty space surrounding it feels perfectly, mathematically balanced within the structural grid.

When to Stop Building

“The project is complete not when there is nothing left to add, but when there is nothing left to take away,” Cross states, referencing the ultimate minimalist ethos. The final day on a site is never spent installing; it is spent quietly removing the last remaining distractions.
