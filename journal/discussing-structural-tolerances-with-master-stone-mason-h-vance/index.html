Discussing Interior Joinery Tolerances With MasterStone Mason H. Vance

A shadow gap of three millimetres reads as intention. At five, it reads as error. We sat down with the mason who has cut stone for four of our commissions to discuss why tolerance, not material, decides whether a wall feels permanent.

Where the Eye Begins to Doubt

“Nobody measures a joint,” Vance says. “But everybody feels one. The moment a reveal wanders by a couple of millimetres across a ten-metre run, the whole wall stops reading as a single piece of stone and starts reading as assembly.”

The Cost of a Millimetre

Tightening a tolerance from five millimetres to two can double the cutting time on a facade. Vance is unsentimental about this. The expense is not craft for its own sake; it is the difference between a surface the inhabitant trusts and one they unconsciously interrogate.

Reading the Bed Before Cutting

Every block arrives with a grain and a direction of weakness. Vance spends the better part of a day simply looking before the first cut is made. “The stone will tell you where it wants to break. Argue with it and you will be back in six months.”

Why Machines Are Not Enough

Computer-guided saws hold a tolerance a hand cannot match, yet Vance finishes every visible arris by hand. The machine produces an edge that is too perfect, and a too-perfect edge catches light in a way that immediately reads as manufactured rather than quarried.

Movement Is Not Failure

Stone moves with heat and moisture, and a wall detailed without room to move will fail. The tolerances we specify are not only about appearance; they are the allowance that lets a monolithic surface survive four decades of thermal cycling without a visible fracture.

The Joint as Ornament

Where we have removed all applied decoration, the joint becomes the only ornament available. Its rhythm, its depth, and its shadow carry the visual interest that a cornice once carried. This is why the studio treats setting-out as a design act rather than a site activity.

Training the Next Hand

Vance has taken two apprentices in thirty years. “You cannot teach tolerance in a classroom. You teach it by making somebody cut the same stone eleven times until they stop asking why the tenth one was not good enough.”

What Permanence Actually Costs

Asked what clients most misunderstand, Vance does not hesitate. “They think permanence is a material. It is not. It is a tolerance, held by somebody who refuses to let it slip on a Friday afternoon.”
